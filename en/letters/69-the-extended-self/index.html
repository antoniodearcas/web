Nº 69 · June 29, 2025

The "Extended Self"

Please, call me by my true names

Vidriera con módulo Hele - Rafael Leoz

Someone in my family asked me the other day: If love works so well, why is it so hard for us to love more?

My immediate response was: “Do you see yourself as an individual separate from the rest?”

That answer points to the conclusion, so let’s start from the beginning.

Before humans, the first sign of loving behavior on this planet was between mothers and their offspring, especially in mammals. This is because animals consider their young as part of themselves—as an extended self. Their sense of identity extends to other bodies, which they now protect as their own.

I remember witnessing this one day, watching a mother with her child sitting next to me at the airport. The child was playing with the bench, and his mother was looking at him curiously, not quite understanding what he was doing. When she finally saw that the boy was trying to crawl through the armrest, the mother said: “Ah, so that’s what we’re doing!”

We might say that’s just a manner of speaking, but monkeys and mice don’t speak and they exhibit the same extended self. From this, we could define love as recognizing the other as part of your being.

This scientific observation is not far from the non-dual reality that all religions point toward. The key to love is that extended self. We could measure our capacity to love by how wide and deep our extended self is.

The question is: Who do I include in my extended self?

That depends on what I identify with. If it’s my last name, my extended self will include my family. If it’s my nation, it will extend to my compatriots. Soccer teams, religions, music styles, mother tongues, clothing brands, sexual orientations, skin colors… Identifying with any of these will, on the one hand, make us feel part of a group, making us feel loved, understood, and welcomed. On the other hand, it will exclude from our extended self anyone who is not part of that group. This is the essence of tribalism. It is a subtle act of violence with powerful effects. Every arbitrary label we assume as “me” is a seed of war.

Why don’t we love more, if it’s always so beneficial?

Because to love, we must stop identifying with those labels, or at least set them aside, consciously prioritizing what irrevocably unites us. I may be left-wing and you right-wing, we may belong to opposing nations, but beyond all that, you are my brother. Many have chosen to die before admitting this.

To love requires looking beyond the surface and recognizing that, in essence, we are all the same. We all have an ego that makes us violent toward ourselves and toward others. We all carry within us the same divine essence that can undo the ego. We all seek happiness and work to secure its sources in our lives. We all seek to relieve our suffering and remove its causes. We all do this 24 hours a day.

To love requires looking more deeply, knowing oneself on such an essential level that we begin to recognize that same essence in others, regardless of superficial differences. On the surface, the sea appears as a multitude of waves that rise up, take different shapes moment by moment, and eventually disappear. All is chaos and agitation, whirlpools and foam. Yet when we dive in search of the essence, just a few meters down we find nothing but a uniform and seemingly infinite mass of water. To recognize this is to remember our eternal state of communion with all the waves, and with the ocean itself.

It’s not about learning to love; it’s about unveiling the falseness of everything that is not love. That is all we must do. Love will arise on its own once the obstacles fall away.

Since I’ve been exploring Buddhism lately, I’ll end with a beautiful poem written by the monk Thich Nhat Hanh, written from the awareness of an oceanic “extended self”:

Call Me by My True Names

by Thich Nhat Hanh

Do not say that I'll depart tomorrow
because even today I still arrive.

Look deeply: I arrive in every second
to be a bud on a spring branch,
to be a tiny bird, with wings still fragile,
learning to sing in my new nest,
to be a caterpillar in the heart of a flower,
to be a jewel hiding itself in a stone.

I still arrive, in order to laugh and to cry,
to fear and to hope.
The rhythm of my heart is the birth and
death of all that is alive.

I am the mayfly metamorphosing on the surface of the river,
and I am the bird which, when spring comes, arrives in time
to eat the mayfly.

I am the frog swimming happily in the clear water of a pond,
and I am also the grass-snake who, approaching in silence,
feeds itself on the frog.

I am the child in Uganda, all skin and bones,
my legs as thin as bamboo sticks,
and I am the arms merchant, selling deadly weapons to Uganda.

I am the twelve-year-old girl, refugee on a small boat,
who throws herself into the ocean after being raped by a sea pirate,
and I am the pirate, my heart not yet capable of seeing and loving.

I am a member of the politburo, with plenty of power in my hands,
and I am the man who has to pay his "debt of blood" to my people,
dying slowly in a forced labor camp.

My joy is like spring, so warm it makes flowers bloom in all walks of life.
My pain is like a river of tears, so vast it fills the four oceans.

Please call me by my true names,
so I can hear all my cries and my laughter at once,
so I can see that my joy and pain are one.

Please call me by my true names,
so I can wake up
and the door of my heart can be left open,
the door of compassion.

So I return to the initial question: Do you consider yourself an individual separate from the rest?
Who do you include in your extended self?
And above all: Who do you exclude?

With all my love,

A.
