45. The Inner Master

Revealing the creativity that we already are

"Your task is not to seek love, but to search for and find all the barriers within yourself that you have built against it." - Rumi

When I first arrived at Berklee, I had an intuition that made me take a 180º turn in my path right from the start. It was my first semester, and my peers and I were each embarking on our own journeys, all with the same goal: to become a better version of ourselves as artists.

The intuition I had during that first semester was this: I am not a mediocre artist working to improve; I am already the master composer I seek. That infinite creative force is already within me in all its glory, and my work is to allow it to express itself freely.

My journey at Berklee began like everyone else’s, taking classes designed to accumulate information and, hopefully, some knowledge. But little by little, I started to veer toward another path—one focused on unlearning and letting go. That’s why I sought out every course related to spirituality that Berklee offered. Spirituality is not about becoming something better; it’s about letting go of what blocked the light you already were from the beginning.

Now, as I start my final year, I have finally accepted that my path at Berklee is a spiritual journey, not an academic one. I still have “required” classes (about jazz history or entrepreneurship), but my real work lies in the 10 hours of qigong practice I’ve signed up for each week. That’s where I cultivate the creative energy (which expresses itself, for instance, in this letter) and the awareness that helps me discover and dissolve the ways in which I sabotage its flow.

Those 10 hours of clearing clouds have already let in a remarkable amount of light—a light that has allowed me to see this concept of the inner master with greater clarity. That’s what I want to share today.

The substance we are made of is creativity itself; it is qi, the primordial energy from which all Creation is made. This creative energy can generate anything we are capable of imagining. Here lies both our problem and our salvation. It is up to our mind to determine whether our life becomes a hell or heaven itself.

If your conscious mind says, “I’m terrible in the kitchen,” the original qi says, “Yes,” and creates that reality for you.

If your mind says, “My stomach is upset,” the original qi says, “Of course, here’s some pain.”

If your mind says, “I’m creatively blocked,” the original qi stops bringing songs and instead generates the energy of frustration, which is precisely what you’ve requested with your prayer: “I’m creatively blocked.”

A possible solution to this is positive affirmations. Thinking positive thoughts has a positive effect to the extent that other negative thoughts and energies don’t cancel them out. Imagine a completely cloudy sky. Using positive affirmations is like shooting at the clouds. Yes, each bullet will create a tunnel through which a ray of light can shine, but the clouds will soon close again.

This is why the most serious and powerful approach is to work with the clouds first. Our true nature is the luminous sky behind them, and to the extent that we remove the clouds, that luminous nature will express itself effortlessly.

I’m increasingly understanding why that intuition I had during my first semester was correct. The other day, I watched the new Bob Dylan movie. It shows Bob, prettier than ever, composing brilliant songs almost with urgency; the creative force wanted to flow through him, and his job was simply to allow it. He says he wrote Blowin’ in the Wind in 10 minutes, and I believe him. Bob Dylan didn’t write those incredible songs through effort; he wrote them from a place of power, in a state of ecstatic inspiration.

Bob Dylan was someone whose identity in no way opposed the universal creative force. It wasn’t a matter of pride; it was certainty. He knew he had the capacity to write extraordinary songs and accepted that fact naturally: “This is who I am.”

In my first semester, I recognized that fact. I called it “the master within me”, though I didn’t yet understand the impersonal nature of that creative force. And I also recognized the ways in which the identity I had created opposed its expression. That’s when this journey began.

But it is only a journey from the perspective of that identity—this small self, full of dark clouds, repeatedly facing its own extinction. From the perspective of the master, who I truly am in essence, there is no journey. The sun is not going anywhere, and always shines equally, whether our world is cloudy or clear.

Most of the time I forget who I truly am and believe I am the cloud and the shadow it casts. I am like Frodo, frightened and exhausted in his epic journey to Mordor. But sometimes I remember who I am, and then I compose joyfully, or sing while climbing the stairs, my shoulders relax, and I smile. Then I direct my thoughts knowing that by doing so, I am creating a more harmonious and beautiful reality, and I give thanks for my existence.

All of this can be summed up in something my qigong master always tells new students in their first class: “You came to Berklee to become something, but guess what? You’re not becoming anything! You’re just revealing what you already are by removing the obstacles to its awareness.”

Our responsibility is not to seek love in some other place or future time, as if something were missing here and now. Our only responsibility is to love now. Everything else will follow naturally. The Inner Master will take care of it.

With all my love,

A.
