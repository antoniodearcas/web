"What is", and you

Conversation on the way to oneness

64. “What is”, and you

This semester I had a very powerful experience, which I already shared in another letter. I was in a qigong class, doing an exercise called Lachi, which consists of holding a ball of energy between your hands, expanding and contracting it ad infinitum, cultivating and refining the energy. The simplicity of the exercise allowed me to go deeper and let my mind expand.

The first thing I saw was that I was not the body. The body was like a puppet following the energy, like a flag follows the wind. But then, after about 30 minutes in that state, I asked myself, “And who is moving the energy?” That’s when I recognized that what I am is neither the body nor the energy, but the will that directs it all. Then a new question arose: Is this my will, or God’s will? What is the relationship between the two?

Let’s explore.

The Will of God can be defined as “what is,” because, being infinite in power, if God says something shall be, it is: “Let there be light,” and there is light. This also implies that the Will of God is Truth, since only “what is” can be true. Parmenides put it like this: “What is, is; what is not, is not.”

Once someone asked Meister Eckhart, “What is God?” and he simply replied, “Isness” — that is, the very quality of being. This removes both the past (which no longer is) and the future (which is not yet) from the equation. There is nothing real there, nothing of value for the one who seeks truth. Therefore, the Will of God is the eternal present. But not the fleeting phenomena we observe in time. God, being eternal, only creates eternal things, and thus His will cannot refer to the changing things of this world. In short, the Will of God is eternally present and is the only thing that is real.

Now, my individual will doesn’t seem quite so… effective. I laugh as I write this. Sometimes I say, “Don’t finish that plate of lentils — you’re already full!” and the next thing I know, I’m licking the plate clean. Other times I write a to-do list for the day, and by nightfall I haven’t checked off a single thing.

Moreover, my individual will doesn’t always seem to be aimed at the present moment. Right now it is: my will is to write, and I’m writing. But often it’s oriented toward the future: “my will is to write every day,” for example. That’s when things start to wobble. Limiting my will to the present moment would mean completely giving up the intention to control the future. That would require infinite trust. The ego doesn’t like that idea. The ego, being rooted in fear, is incapable of trusting. Instead, it has a bunch of preferences, always contradictory, through which it tries to manipulate the future. This leads to a will that has no idea what it wants.

This is what I’ve been observing very closely these past few months: my will is fragmented. And that fragmentation also fractures my sense of identity. Who am I — the one who wants to finish the plate of lentils, or the one who doesn’t? In that moment, my mind seems split into two or more factions, and conflict becomes inevitable.

We can still function in the world without resolving this competition between wills. That’s what we call effort — or willpower. If it takes effort to get out of bed in the morning, it’s because you want to get up and, at the same time, you don’t. If exercising feels like a struggle, it’s because one part of your mind says “yes!” and another says “no!” So the only way a divided mind can move things forward is through constant effort. That tension, maintained moment by moment, consumes a great deal of energy. It’s like driving with the parking brake on. “Yes, but no.”

Another effect of this fragmented will is perpetual dissatisfaction. In this world, we might be more or less dissatisfied, but complete satisfaction is much harder to attain. A truly satisfied person would be someone whose every desire has been fulfilled — each and every one, exactly as they desired. Only someone whose will has become totally unified with God’s could say they are completely satisfied, entirely free from suffering — because, at that point, what they want is identical to “what is.”

But most of us are not there yet. The ego tells us we have an individual will — it calls it “free will” — and that surrendering to the Will of God would mean abandoning that freedom. But the ego is, quite literally, the world turned upside down. Notice that this position is based on a denial of “what is”. Only by denying the reality of God — of unity, peace, and love — can the ego say: “I am something separate, and my freedom depends on maintaining that supposed independence.” But what if freedom isn’t what the ego says it is? The ego says freedom means having a thousand options and no one telling you what to do. But what if free will — true freedom — is actually the Will of God, and the ego is just an illusion keeping us chained to a state of conflict?

Let’s take a closer look at the ego’s idea of “freedom.” The ego, in truth, has no will — only impossible desires. Its desires always involve trying to change “what is”, which is ontologically impossible. Its very starting point is a denial of reality. This may well be the epitome of the saying, “what begins badly, ends badly.”

These desires, moreover, contradict one another — and some are very, very dark. Freud, for instance, identified Thanatos, the death drive. That, and other such “delights,” is what we’ve hidden in the subconscious because we don’t want to identify with it. But pushing something into the subconscious inevitably means projecting it outward. It’s the perverse reverse of the Golden Rule: giving others what we don’t want for ourselves. From that point on, we live in a kind of dark dream. We’ve not only hidden what we truly are — we’ve also eclipsed others behind a veil of projections. That dream is not “what is”, which is now hidden. That dream is “what ends badly.”

But it doesn’t end there. As John Lennon said, “Everything will be okay in the end. If it’s not okay, it’s not the end.” Behind the veil woven by the ego’s projections, Heaven waits patiently. Jesus put it this way: “The Kingdom of God is spread out upon the Earth, but men do not see it.” (Gospel of Thomas, saying 113) And the body doesn’t have to die for us to reach it. Another way of naming Heaven — or the Kingdom of God — is “what is.” And that is present; it is, in fact, the Present itself.

The promise is this: if we restore the unity of our will, the resulting will shall be the Will of God, and in Him we will recognize our Self. Jesus said it like this: “I and the Father are one.” In that unity, “Know thyself” and “Thy will be done” converge. One will, one Self.

So then — who are you?

We’ve said that the Will of God has nothing to do with time, nor with the passing phenomena of this world. Therefore, the Will of God is not that you do this or that in the world. It’s not that you change anything in this world. It’s not that you change at all. How could that which is already perfect and eternal need to change?

The Will of God is not something you “have to do” — it’s something you already are. It’s not something you must attain, but something waiting to be recognized. It is the recognition and acceptance of your true nature: you are one with Him and with all creation. God’s Will for you is perfect peace, love, and joy.

Peace, because what is One knows no conflict.

Love, because it knows no separation.

And joy, because — how could you frown where there is perfect peace and endless love?

A Course in Miracles puts it this way: “God’s Will for you is perfect happiness.” To the extent that I recognize that I am peace, love, and perfect happiness — and not the one who suffers (that’s the ego) — I am accepting the Will of God.

If I want to return to reality, I must accept that it’s not up to me to decide anything.

I simply must be with “what is.”

I must be “what is.”

I am that I am.

And that’s as far as language can take us. Thank you for walking with me to the end of words. Beyond that, there is only silence — and perhaps some good music.

With all my love,

A.
