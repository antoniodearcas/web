Nº 54

The name "I"

That we all share

Did anyone ever explain to you that you should refer to yourself as “I” and not by your own name? Probably not—I, at least, don’t remember ever being confused about that. Everyone called me by my name, but I started my sentences with “I.”

I am hungry.

I want to sleep a little more.

Curiously, if instead of referring to myself as “I,” I replace the subject with “Antonio,” suddenly the sentence is no longer in the first person, but in the third.

“Antonio is hungry” instead of “Antonio am hungry.”

If I refer to myself as Antonio, I’m speaking about something separate from me—something that is not strictly me. Only “I” is first person.

If words are signs that point to realities, then “I” and my proper name do not point to the same reality—they are not the same thing.

The proper name “Antonio” creates separation—it’s a label, an external reference, it points to a mask. In fact, the word person comes from the Greek πρόσωπον (prósōpon), which means “mask.” Antonio is the person.

On the other hand, “I” points to something entirely true and intimate—something real. What is real is what does not change. Whatever I am, I am I, I have always been I, and I always will be I. The reality that “I” points to is constant—prior to all the changes that Antonio might go through. Only “I” has the right to the first person; only “I” points to the center of my being and is the true subject of my life.

Being the most intimate name, it is also the most universal. All human beings in the world (except for James Brown) refer to themselves with this one unique and mysteriously shared name.

There is a profound, metaphysical wisdom in language—and not just in ours, probably in all of them.

Now, a leap in time.

Moses, after going through a great ego-death (he realizes he’s Hebrew, not Egyptian—and on top of that, he kills a man) and spending 40 years in the desert (the ultimate low-information diet), has a mind more open and receptive than the bowl that holds the universe.

One day, while walking with his sheep, he comes across a burning bush that reveals itself as God. God gives him a mission, and when Moses asks what he should say when people ask who sent him, God replies: “I Am that I Am.”

Reality has an identity, and it knows itself as “I.” “I” is God’s first name—the most sacred and mysterious of all. “I” is the subjective element of existence, which all of us who exist share.

At one level, the phrase “Hallowed be thy name” refers to “God,” to “Yahweh,” or any proper name with which we try to name ultimate reality. But at a deeper, more immediate and mysterious level, I believe “Hallowed be thy name” points directly to the name “I”—the most sacred and intimate name of God, which He shares with us. It’s an invitation to recognize that the divine, the sacred, is not far from our earthly existence—it dwells at the very center of our being.

The way to profane the name “I” is to equate it with “Antonio”, believing that what I am shares the qualities of a body: limited, separate, mortal. The way to sanctify it is to restore its divine status: what I am is unlimited, one with all that is, eternal.

What I try to explain in a thousand words, the Sufis (the mystical branch of Islam) express in one sentence:

“Whoever knows himself, knows his Lord.”

And there I leave you, with a quote and an identity crisis.

But crises are good. Leaving behind obsolete structures opens space for new beginnings, more creative, authentic and wise paths. This is how nature renews itself and moves forward, with each passing Spring. And that is how we renew and advance ourselves, letting Spring pass through us as well. Something new wants to be born.

With all my love,

A.
