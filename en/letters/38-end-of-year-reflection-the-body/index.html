End-of-Year Reflection: The Body

5 questions about each area of your life to end the year with clarity

THE BODY

1. What kind of movement or exercise has brought me the most joy or sense of freedom this year?

2. Do I feel better than I did a year ago? What has changed in my body?

3. Is my body trying to tell me something through sensations or pain? What can I do to help it?

4. What am I most grateful for about my body this year?

5. Have I allowed myself enough rest? If not, what took priority over it?

REFLECTION

After finishing high school, I stopped exercising. Chema, the gym teacher, no longer came on Tuesdays to make me run. No one came to replace him, and I stopped running or doing any other type of exercise. At that stage of my life, I remember talking about my body as merely a vehicle for my mind. I didn’t value it or understood how grateful it is when you show it a little love, and as a result, I didn’t give it any.

At 21, I started experiencing back and hip pain. The body speaks through sensations. Pain points to a specific place and says, "Look here, something isn’t right; you need to change something." My sister joined a yoga studio that was literally a 15-second walk from my house—there was no excuse. That’s where I began to get to know my body, and to know is to love. The pain disappeared quickly.

That marked the beginning of an 8-year period during which movement and exercise slowly entered my life more consistently. My understanding of exercise was that it was something I needed to do to feel good. Once you experience your body in good shape, you can’t settle for anything less. During those years, I exercised for the benefits it gave me, and that’s great.

But this year, my perspective has shifted to something much better. This year, my body was speaking to me again, loud and clear. I was exercising quite regularly, but there were things in my body that seemed to continue deteriorating. One day, I understood that exercise could no longer be optional; it had to be a non-negotiable part of my days. I had made that commitment many times intellectually—it came from the ego. This time, it happened in my heart—it came from the spirit.

Shortly after, I made a new friend who offered to train me in exchange for guitar lessons. In our first session, he simply guided me through a breathing exercise. But in those brief 15 minutes, I understood something profound.

All my life, I had been exercising for reasons external to the act of exercising itself. I practiced yoga or lifted weights to enjoy the results. That day, I realized that wasn’t enough—that the true way to integrate exercise into my life was to do it for the sake of the action itself. That day, I found joy in simply breathing. A few days later, I deeply enjoyed each stroke while swimming. And one morning, doing hip mobility exercises, I was moved to the brink of tears by the joy of just being in my body, helping it. The reward lies in the work itself, not in the results. If you’re focused on the results, you’re not present in the action, and therefore, you’ve closed off the source of joy, which opens only when you set your full attention in the present moment.

Now, when I exercise, I am simultaneously meditating. It’s a completely different experience to lift a weight while thinking about something else versus lifting a weight while fully immersed in lifting the weight. The challenge with this approach is that exercise involves physical discomfort, and we don’t like that—so we resist being present with those sensations. But if you relax and invite the sensation instead of pushing it away, an incredible joy and beauty emerge.

A Zen anecdote comes to mind. A Zen student asks his master, “Master, what is the difference between you and me?” The master replies, “When you eat, walk, or wash dishes, you do so while thinking about other things. When I eat, I eat. When I walk, I walk. And when I wash the dishes, I wash the dishes.”

When you exercise, exercise. The true reward will then reveal itself.

With all my love,

A.
