Nº 81 · September 21, 2025

Anatomy of a Crisis

How well do you fit in your chosen pot?

Imagine a plant growing in a pot. If it is meant to grow large, sooner or later that initial pot will become too small. If the gardener is skillful, he will recognize the moment when the plant needs more space and will transplant it into a larger pot so the roots can continue expanding. If the gardener is absent, the plant may break the pot if its roots are strong, but more often it will simply wither from lack of nutrients and space.

Something similar happens to us human beings. The pot we live in is the context from which we conceive of our existence and who we are. The smaller the context, the greater our sense of control will be—but the fewer possibilities for real growth.

The widest possible context is the Ultimate Reality, also known as the Divine. Living in that context consciously is like a plant living out in nature, with all the space, water, sun, and nutrients it could ever need to grow. Reality is infinite, ungraspable, and ineffable. The Tao that can be named is not the true Tao of all.

To fully accept that context and be completely coherent with it implies total surrender. It is letting go of control entirely, or rather, letting go of the illusion that we ever had control. It is the sage acknowledging that he truly knows nothing. It is Jesus in Gethsemane saying: “Not my will, but yours be done.” It is the ice cube dissolving into the ocean. It is surrendering to the flow of the River and letting what must happen, happen, without clinging or resisting.

Most of us struggle to accept that context, not only because it is hard to understand, but above all because of the letting go of control. Fear quickly comes to our “rescue.” Yet our civilization is founded on the teachings of those who surrendered to that extreme, for only a model based on Reality can truly work.

What most of us do is choose a pot to live in instead. Some inherit their pot from their parents or culture without ever asking whether it might be too narrow. Others read a few books and decide to move into a different pot from those around them. But in the end, all pots are still pots. Just as we feel safer swimming in a pool than in the middle of the ocean, we feel safer in a pot than in the open field. The problem is that the tree we are is destined to be very large, and no matter what pot we put ourselves in, sooner or later it will be too small. What happens when that moment comes?

The most common thing is that the part of us that wanted to grow, upon encountering the limits of the pot, simply accepts that limit and stops pushing. Then the teaching of the sage Dylan applies: “That which is not busy being born is busy dying.” Let’s look at a few examples.

If I put myself in the pot of “I am a shy person,” then every time life offers me an opportunity to show up, to shine, or to offer value, my spirit will bump against that “pot,” and if it is not strong enough, it will choose to surrender to that apparent wall. This is the origin of neuroses. The great psychoanalyst Carl Jung described it this way:

“I have frequently seen people become neurotic when they content themselves with inadequate or wrong answers to the questions of life. Such people are usually confined within too narrow a spiritual horizon. If they are enabled to develop into more spacious personalities, the neurosis generally disappears.”

Or I may put myself in the pot of “I am an engineer, that’s how I make a living, it’s a safe and respected path.” What, then, happens to my spirit that longs to write and sing songs? If I yield to the wall and to fear rather than face the unknown, that branch will simply never grow, and perhaps the tree I am will never bear the fruits it could have borne.

But what if the spirit that seeks to expand is strong and decides to move through fear, trusting that this impulse will carry it toward the good? What happens then is that the stronger roots will push against the pot until it breaks. That is when the crisis occurs.

The word crisis comes from the ancient Greek κρίσις (krísis), meaning “separation, decision, judgment.” It is an order which, being too narrow to contain the vital impulse, ends up breaking and leaving one exposed to the unknown. At that point, there is nothing left but to analyze the situation and make the most informed judgment possible about what we want to do next.

All crises are painful because they confront us with chaos, where our most basic survival instincts are triggered: fear, anxiety, attachment to the known, the desire for control… Yet they also bring the possibility of extraordinary growth if we have the vision and the courage to seize the infinite potential that chaos offers. We can renew our lives, aligning them with the broadest context we are capable of conceiving.

That is why, after 10 years of frequent crises, I have come to develop a true appreciation for these moments of rupture with the known. One begins to see the deeper pattern: fear and all survival instincts are useful only up to a certain point. But if our Spirit calls us to more than a life of mere survival, we can choose to trust It, learning to listen and to be guided. We can abandon all the limited models we have built to replace Reality. We can stop putting ourselves into prefabricated pots that will sooner or later become too small, and finally allow ourselves to be trees growing in the open wilderness. The Reality that created us knows our needs better than we do. Only in that context can we live without fear, free to expand and be all that we are.

Now, at the beginning of this letter I mentioned the presence of a gardener. His absence leads to the two dramatic outcomes: withering, or waiting for the crisis to explode violently. But if the gardener is present and skillful, he will know when it is time to transplant into a larger pot. He will get his hands dirty, yes, but it will be a beautiful and liberating process.

We are the plant, but also the gardener. The gardener is the part of you that already knows what the plant needs. We cannot lose that voice, because it is our true voice; but we can stop hearing it if we give more power to the voices of fear or laziness. Then we experience the absence of the gardener. Yet deep down you already know what you truly want and what you must do. To that skillful gardener within you I ask: In what pot have you placed yourself that is now becoming too small? What is within you that longs to grow, but cannot find the space to do so? The solution will not come from outside, but from you, from your capacity to speak the truth and align yourself with it despite what fear may say. The Ultimate Reality offers us Everything, but we fail to recognize it because we hide within the apparent safety that a pot provides. Ask yourself:

What story of fear or limitation am I believing?

Could I let it go?

Would I let it go?

When?

With all my love,

A.
