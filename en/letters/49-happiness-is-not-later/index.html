49. Happiness is not "later"

This instant is a portal to Heaven, waiting to be opened.

If this moment were the only one you would experience for the rest of eternity, how would you hold it? What would your internal posture be?

Consider this for a second. This snapshot of your life, frozen forever. And yet, you would still be in it—alive, breathing, contemplating that static universe.

This question appeared in my mind during a meditation this week. I must have been halfway through when I started growing impatient. It was in the morning, I was hungry, and I wanted to finish meditating so I could finally enjoy my well-deserved and much-anticipated bowl of oatmeal with chocolate, banana, blueberries, and walnuts. “Hmmm,” my mind mused absentmindedly, counting the blueberries. I mean… the minutes.

Then this question struck my mind like lightning, confronting me with infinity: this moment, exactly as it is—with this hunger, the slight discomfort in my body—everything, just as it is now, sitting here, forever. That bowl would never come.

My first reaction was terror, like a kind of claustrophobia in time rather than in space. But that cloud passed quickly, and what happened next was mysterious. The inevitable answer was: "I’m not going to resist it for all eternity, so sooner or later, I will accept and open myself completely to this moment." And, as if sliding toward the truth almost without realizing it, that’s exactly what happened.

Then something like an explosion in my consciousness occurred—an opening to a joy and peace so overwhelming that something within me got scared, and I shut myself off again from that luminous and boundless present.

It was a glimpse of a radical truth: happiness is not "later." It is here or nowhere. This moment is absolutely complete—nothing is missing—and in fact, it brings so many gifts that I am not even prepared to accept all that it offers.

I realized how, just seconds earlier, I had been projecting the source of happiness into the future—this particular time, onto a bowl of oatmeal. “There’s what will finally make me feel satisfied, not here” was the hidden, absurd, and frustrating thought that my mind held onto, as it always does whenever I am not in the ecstasy of communion with God, which doesn’t happen so frequently.

That is what we do all the time: resist this moment, to some degree or another, waiting for something supposedly better to arrive later.

And so, we weave the illusion of time—an endless transition toward something that never comes. And it never comes because we are looking in the wrong place. We believe the end of suffering will arrive in the future—when a certain person changes, when the sun comes out, when there is a good government, when I earn more money, when my body heals… but that search is suffering itself. That search is a denial of the truth, and therefore, it pushes it out of our awareness. And then we’re left feeling thirsty. If we were to abandon the search and accept this moment as if it were the only one we would ever have, all the problems we perceive would dissolve into an ocean of fresh water.

But it doesn’t stop there—I kept pulling the thread:

Pain is that which happens in the present moment, calling out to us, saying, “Hey! Look here!” At the same time, pain is that which we avoid at all costs, that which we run away from.

When that question appeared in my mind, my suffering was minimal: I was a little hungry and slightly bored. Even so, when faced with the idea of fully accepting that moment, I resisted—even felt terror.

But what if that question had come to me during a truly painful moment? A moment of feeling rejected and humiliated, a moment of intense physical pain? That is truly terrifying, and accepting it fully would require inconceivable courage. How would I hold that painful moment if it were all I were to experience for eternity?

Then I remembered Jesus Christ, and I understood that this is exactly what He did in His crucifixion. It is hard to imagine a moment more painful than that. Most likely, we will never have to endure anything even remotely similar. And yet, He said “Yes” completely—not with resignation, but with total surrender. He did not merely accept that moment; He embraced it with an open heart. Not a trace of resentment—only love.

When I go to this point, my mind melted and I fell into my heart, returning to that communion. I felt the presence of Christ, as if He were nodding at this newfound understanding He had brought me, and He showed me something I had never seen before: infinite courage.

Christ on the cross is infinite courage—the capacity for total surrender, the ability to say a universal “Yes.” That capacity is within me, latent, waiting to be invoked, because Christ is within me, latent, waiting to be invoked.

I pause now and return to contemplating that initial question. What made me close myself off to its answer? Why did I become afraid when confronted by its light? On one hand, there is the fear of the unknown, but there is more to it than that. If that question is a door, the key is forgiveness. There are things within me that I have not yet forgiven. I know this because I still often see the world as dark and threatening.

When I asked that question, the door opened a millimeter, and the light threatened to reveal something that does not want to be seen—something that thinks, "If I am discovered, I will lose my dominion;" something that feels, "If I am discovered, I will never be loved again."

That is why Christ teaches forgiveness and, at the same time, offers His infinite courage. Dare to look within, let that which is hidden be seen, and I will dissolve it in My love so that it may never frighten you again. And so, little by little, there will be fewer and fewer of those [whatever they are] trying to slam shut the heaven within me.

Perhaps one day, I will open the door, accept that infinitely radiant welcome, and step inside. I’ll let you know!

With all my love,

A.
