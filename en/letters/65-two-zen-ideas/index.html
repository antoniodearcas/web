65. Two Zen Ideas

How to breathe. How to love

Today I want to share two ideas that a friend shared with me this week. Their path is within the Zen tradition, so it was a refreshing break from my own mystical-Christian densities. The intention of this letter is just that — a breath of fresh air.

And with that, the first idea, which has to do with the breath. When meditating, instead of simply paying attention to the breath, we can conceive of it this way: the body is the house, attention is the host, and the breath is the guest.

The breath is coming and going all the time, and our only job is to accompany it with our attention. As it enters, we welcome it. As it leaves, we thank it.

The breath is what keeps us connected to life. It is how life animates our body. Each breath is a divine gift.

But it doesn’t stop there. Sitting in a room full of other meditators, this exercise eventually leads to the awareness that everyone in the room is breathing the same life. We are all in different houses, but the life that animates us is the same for all. We are immersed in it like fish in water. With a shift in understanding, we can recognize that neither we nor the others are the house, but life itself — one, universal, shared. It’s a Zen way of entering into communion with others.

The second idea comes from a book by Thich Nhat Hanh called How to Love. In it, he shares four mantras that we can use to cultivate a more conscious and compassionate relationship with someone we love. The four mantras are:

“I am here for you.”

“I know you are there, and I am very happy.”

“I know you suffer. That is why I am here for you.”

“I am suffering. Please help me.”

I recommend practicing this with someone you love and trust. The idea is to look at the other person and say the four phrases from the heart, savoring them, observing the reaction in the other and in oneself after each one. The phrases are powerful, but their true power lies in the sincerity and presence with which they are spoken.

I tried it today with Betül, and we both ended up with tears in our eyes. In particular, it was saying the last phrase that opened, like a magic key, something that had been locked away and wanted to come out. What a relief to stop being “strong” and open ourselves to radical vulnerability.

Following the Asian thread, I’ll share one final thought in Taoist style:

He who appears invulnerable is the one who suffers most.
To show oneself vulnerable is to remember our invulnerability.

With all my love,
A.
