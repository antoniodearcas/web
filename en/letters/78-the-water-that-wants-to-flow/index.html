78. The Water That Wants to Flow

On your will and its obstacles

Buddha described laziness as anything that prevents us from moving in the direction we want to move, or from doing what we feel called to do. He spoke of three kinds of laziness, each subtler and harder to identify. That’s why seeing them described so clearly is valuable. Here they are:

  1. Jéluk gi lélo: attachment to sleeping, to “resting,” to free time, or the longing to have nothing to do. This is the laziness we all know, the laziness of indolence: the resistance to doing what we know is beneficial, like meditating, studying, or practicing the piano or the Dharma. It’s driven by inertia, habit, or the desire for immediate pleasure.

  2. Jawa ngenshen gyi lélo: attachment to activity and worldly distractions. Being constantly busy, but with insignificant things. Filling our life with tasks, distractions, overwork, or even good outward actions, but without true attention, pure motivation, or connection with the path. This laziness steals time and energy from what is essential.

  3. Daknyi nyépé lélo: the habit of self-denigration, low self-esteem, self-hatred, and defeatism. It is the laziness of discouragement. Giving up before even trying. Using our low self-esteem to withdraw, to dissociate, to avoid having to rise to the occasion and do what must be done. We use this tendency as an excuse to sink even deeper into the hole we are already in, and then sink even further still. As this state of being becomes more familiar, the “I can’t” identity becomes more comfortable. Finally, we start to resist any action that might counteract the identity we’ve developed of daknyi nyépé, or self-denigration.

Reading this can be a bit tough at first. At least it was for me, especially recognizing to what extent I had fallen into the third type. But now I’m aware of what’s happening, that this is not me, but a mental habit. Now I can disidentify with it. We perpetuate whatever we identify with because, supposedly, our survival depends on it. That’s why we cling to our problems, to our supposed defects, to our narratives of failure, insufficiency, illness, shame, guilt…

But these types of laziness, and the identities we may have built around them, are not us. They are only mental habits, and just as they were once formed unconsciously, now they can be undone consciously. Now that we are aware, we can work with them.

This morning I got up at 6, sat in my armchair feeling my body’s stiffness after the disorder of summer. My will seemed divided: on one hand, I had gotten up at that hour to resume my practices: yoga, qigong, and meditation. On the other, I wanted to go back to bed and sleep three more hours.

What did I do with that conflict? I prayed—or more precisely, I said my daily lesson from A Course in Miracles, perfectly timely: “Holding conflicting desires cannot be my will.”

The answer came immediately: the conflict seemed to be “I want to do this and at the same time I don’t.” Faced with that kind of conflict, we give equal weight to both sides, as if it were a division of our will. But the reality is that there is only one will, and in that moment it was to get out of bed and resume those practices I missed so much. My will is the water that wants to flow, consciousness wanting to grow, and the stance of “I don’t want to do it, for whatever reason” is the resistance to the course of life. There are not two wills, only “will” and “resistance to will.”

Seeing this, it only took me an instant to jump from the armchair, grab my mat, and start doing what it was my will to do.

We are the water that wants to flow. What brings us closer to a state of vitality, balance, joy, health, strength, and calm is our true will. The rest are natural resistances, like the rocks in a river—not personalities to punish or repress.

And now, time to meditate.

With all my love,

A.
