84. The Ninth Garden

In a country where it barely rains, a young architect feels called to create a garden. After a year of work, he finally inaugurates it under the name The Garden of Silence. A land without life or water, yet full of beauty and harmony: rocks, minerals, and sand arranged with such mastery that one can almost hear music in the silence that reigns there.

After this initial success, he travels the world creating gardens that are increasingly extravagant and wondrous.

His second garden, called The Garden of the Sea, is an enormous expanse of water. There, the simplest forms of life travel with the currents and tides. By day, the surface is painted with colors. By night, life illuminates the waves with its bioluminescence.

The third garden, The Green Garden, includes not only the sea and the land but also the plant world. Algae, moss, lichens, shrubs, trees, and flowering plants cover the ocean floor and the land surface. Given the complexity of this garden, the architect assigns a fortunate gardener to care for his creation. He chooses an expert botanist, trained in the most prestigious gardens, and hands him the keys, saying, “It’s yours now; keep it in harmony.”

The fourth garden, called The Garden of Creatures, is already an intelligent world in itself: elements, plants, and animals of the sea, land, and air share the garden’s circular perimeter. This time, the architect hires botanists, biologists, engineers, shepherds, and all kinds of experts to study and maintain the natural laws governing this unique ecosystem.

For his fifth work, he decides to finally break the already battered concept of a garden and create a natural environment in which humans can live. He approaches an indigenous tribe whose territory was threatened and restores the landscape to idyllic conditions. He names his fifth project The Garden of the Gentle Fire.

For the sixth and most ambitious project yet, he decides to create an entire civilization. For a decade, he builds his sixth garden on the Moon. A city made of dust and rock covers its entire surface. There, a million volunteers learn the language and culture created by the architect, happy to participate in the greatest work of art ever created. Thus, The Gray Garden is born.

For the seventh project, The Lead Garden, he recreates The Green Garden but without relying on organic life. This time, the “plant” world is made up of mechanical microorganisms that organize themselves according to a central artificial intelligence. A bionic nature, identical in appearance and function to the natural world, but entirely mechanical. The flowers are made of aluminum, the mycelium of copper, and everything is powered by the energy generated by millions of green photovoltaic silicon leaves. All that can be heard is an electric hum and the wind whistling like a ghost through the metallic vegetation.

Having pushed the limits of this world to the extreme, his eighth work, The Garden in the Cloud, is purely digital. To access it, visitors must connect their consciousness to a quantum computer that reproduces their bodies inside the garden. Once there, they can shape the world at will, creating seas here and mountains there, like gods giving form to the world.

Thus, he believes he has reached the end of his artistic journey: the recreation of the entire natural world under the dominion of the human mind. Proud, the architect retires and rests for years.

In his old age, the worst nightmare for an architect and gardener begins to threaten him: he is going blind. He goes through a great crisis, which leads him to discover that his blindness has not come to torture him, but to bless him. Now that his eyes grow dark, another kind of vision awakens within him, and his imagination soars higher than ever.

He hears the call to build one final garden, one that transcends the laws of nature, science, and the human mind. This is how he begins his last work, which he temporarily calls The Ninth Garden, without yet revealing its true name.

He decides to return to his homeland, where it rarely rains. Construction begins near a small river, from which he traces a canal that runs through the garden like a central axis. From it branch smaller canals that further subdivide into little irrigation channels. In this way, he creates a watering system to bring water to every plant.

The garden is much humbler than the previous ones, and his team of workers doubts that this is truly the masterpiece that its creator envisions. Yet he continues confidently, and when construction is finished, he sits to listen to the perfection of his work. He hears the gurgle of fountains, the flow of the canals, dragonflies over the pond, the distant cascade, the buzzing of bees among the flowers, and birds in the fruit trees. In his mind, he perfectly sees the entire garden. He knows with absolute precision every corner of it and what every plant and creature needs to thrive in it.

He then decides to hire the person who will take care of his ninth garden. He opens a call for candidates, and on the chosen day, several applicants show up, all prestigious experts in their respective fields. One of them comes with an assistant, a young boy. Hearing his voice, the architect knows he is the right person and announces his choice. The experts cannot believe it; they are outraged and accuse the architect of madness: “This boy is not a candidate, he’s barely out of adolescence! He knows nothing! He will never be able to care for this garden like any of us could.” But the old man reaffirms his decision and, smiling, bids them farewell.

Alone with the young boy, he says: “This garden may seem simple, but it is the most mysterious of all I have created. These men are experts in their fields, and that knowledge blinds them to the mystery that I was only able to glimpse when I went blind. That is why I chose you, with your innocent mind and pure gaze. Don’t worry about not knowing anything yet; your work for now does not require that kind of knowledge. You will live in the little house at the entrance to the park. The garden is not yours, but live in it as if it were. Enjoy it, observe it, and if you find something that seems wrong, like a blocked canal or a plant that looks sad, do not try to do anything on your own; call me, and I will tell you exactly what to do.”

With these words, the architect hands him the keys and leaves. The boy, happy, spends his days walking, sunbathing, whistling with the birds, eating the fruits that grow there, and napping in the shade of the trees. Life is simple and peaceful until one day, a strong wind rises and brings with it a wall of black clouds. Thunder crashes and rain pours down, causing the river to overflow, flooding and destroying the garden with waves of muddy water, rocks, and debris.

Horrified, the boy tries to stop the water, but everything collapses around him. Fearing for his life, he runs to take refuge in the house. A storm also erupts within his inner world, filling him with pain and fear. In his innocent mind, still unable to discern thought from reality, a terrible idea crosses: “The garden was my responsibility, and now it is destroyed. I have failed!” Despite its falsity, the boy accepts the thought as true. In that instant, he loses sight of his innocence and falls into a deep nightmare.

For the first time in his short life, he feels a dagger of guilt pierce his chest. The phone begins to ring, and instead of answering, he unplugs it from the wall. He looks at himself and, seeing his betrayal, shrinks in shame. Now he fears that the architect will come, see the destruction, and unleash his anger not only for ruining the garden but for breaking his trust. His heart freezes. He lowers the blinds, locks the door from the inside, and hides in the darkness, dreaming of punishment.

The storm passes, but the boy does not dare to leave. Someone knocks at the door. It is the architect, calling him by name. The boy runs to hide in a closet and covers his ears to not hear anything. A day passes, then another, and thirst and hunger begin to roar. He is trapped: fear of punishment prevents him from leaving, but without water and food, he will not survive much longer.

Then he remembers a prayer from his childhood and recites it. As he does, something between rage and courage ignites in his chest. The ice cracks. In this new light, he understands that it is better to die with honor at the hands of the architect than to live in fear and hunger in a closet.

He emerges from his hiding place, crosses the house in darkness with firm steps, opens the door, and is blinded by a sun too bright. Someone calls his name. “Here comes death,” he thinks. He opens his arms in a cross, still blinded by the light, surrendering to whatever comes, and exclaims, “I am here!”

And then he receives an embrace. It is the architect, who exclaims with relief, “I thought you had drowned in the river’s flood! What a joy to see you alive! Why didn’t you come out sooner?”

The boy, not understanding, begins to cry. He realizes how wrong he was about the architect and tries to answer: “A terrible storm came, and I didn’t know what to do. The water began to sweep everything away. I tried to save the garden, but it was impossible. I felt that I had completely failed. I hid out of fear that you would punish me, but in the end, I chose to face it rather than stay inside and die of hunger.”

The gardener, moved, replies:

“My dear, I could have chosen experienced, well-trained people to care for the garden. But it would have been destroyed anyway. Brilliant ideas are useless in the face of such a storm.

They too would have felt guilt and shame, believing they had failed, and their fear would have made them hide just as you did. But unlike you, they also had pride, and the storm would have wounded it. That wall would have prevented them from coming out and facing the imagined humiliation. They would have stayed inside to die of hunger before acknowledging the futility of their knowledge.

But you knew nothing, and that is why I chose you. Your innocence gave me the confidence that you would come to me as soon as something went wrong. You did not fail in your task by letting the water destroy everything. That was inevitable. Your only mistake was not to trust in me. You forgot what I told you the first day: simply enjoy and observe, and if something goes wrong, call me.

Following my instructions would have spared you much suffering, and that would have been good. But in your innocence, you were deceived by thoughts of failure and terror. That led you to disobey my instructions. You hid, but something inside you pushed you to come out and face the fear.

And so my true plan for the Ninth Garden was fulfilled.

Today, the seed of the most beautiful flower in this world has sprouted, the only one I never planted in any of my gardens. For many years, I thought it was only a myth. But I discovered it in myself when I went blind and faced my greatest fear. Its beauty amazed me, and I knew I had to dedicate my last work to it.

Today it has bloomed in this garden, and at last I can reveal its true name.

Today you have understood that guilt is nothing but an illusion: it was never your responsibility to know anything about the garden, nor to protect it from the elements as you tried. I only asked that you enjoy it and observe it with your pure gaze.

Today you have also dispelled the shadow of shame, which does not only accuse you of having done something bad, but of being bad yourself. Now you see there was never any real reason to hide.

But above all, today you chose to face fear and confront death with your head held high. In doing so, you discovered that fear is also an illusion. On the other side, only profound relief awaited you.

By understanding my embrace, the flower that gives the garden its name sprouted within you, the most elusive of all, and the one I hoped to cultivate in my last work: The Garden of Wisdom.

But enough talk. I am old, and there is much work ahead. I ask you to be my hands and my eyes. I will guide you and teach you everything you need to know to restore the garden.

Thus, as I teach you to rebuild it, together we will nurture the flower that sprouted in you today. And when the work is done, when the water flows again through the canals and the garden regains its original splendor, the final petal of that flower will open. Then you will know, love, and care for the garden as deeply as I do.”

With all my love,

A.
