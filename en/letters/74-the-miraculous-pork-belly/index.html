74. The Miraculous Pork Belly

That cracked the walls of fear

This letter needs a bit of context.

My relationship with food has always been difficult. Even in my first month of life, I began throwing up my mother’s milk. Since then, digestive issues have followed one another, always in creatively varied forms. It’s been a whole life of associating the act of eating with a sense of danger.

As my consciousness awakens, it begins to open the dark rooms of my mind. Like a torch, it enters them and reveals that the threats suggested by the darkness were nothing but imagination. The truth is light, love, peace, a deep sense of balance, of being safe beyond this world of kaleidoscopic shadows.

A year and a half ago, my consciousness turned its torch toward a large, very dark room: the dining room. I used to enter that space three or more times a day, always in the dark. I always ate distracted, either paying attention to whoever was with me, or, if I was alone, to a book or a screen. But one day, the Guide gently and clearly showed me that I was running away, and that the time had come to step in. That day, I sat down in front of my plate, alone and without distraction. It felt like standing at the mouth of a terrifying cave. But I wasn’t alone. I prayed all my prayers and, holding the Guide’s hand, I began to walk. For the next three weeks, I cried at every meal.

It has been a hard year and a half, as if I were lost in the narrow passages of that cave. At every step, a decision had to be made, a fork in the path. Should I have another spoonful of oats or not? Should I eat meat or better not? Every step I took alone led me deeper into the darkness. Every step I took holding the Guide’s hand came with the promise of bringing me back toward the light. But how distant the light always seemed.

The lessons I’ve received through this process could fill a book. God willing, I’ll write it someday. For now, I want to share one of the latest epiphanies, which came like a hidden pearl inside a piece of fried pork belly. God has no shortage of humor.

Part of the healing process (of my body, but really of my mind) involved taking full responsibility for my situation. No more doctors or nutritionists. No one else can truly know what works for me or what I truly need. Only I can access that information, and for that, I must learn to listen.

For a while, I gathered all kinds of information. I studied macrobiotics, biology, how the body uses different nutrients and which foods contain them. I studied various digestive disorders, different diets, salt, gluten, proteins... Yet this knowledge, or rather, this information, didn’t make me freer; in fact, it only served to further restrict my diet and increase my fear. The reason is that many of these beliefs were contradictory. Is a vegan diet better, or an omnivorous one? Is oats a holy remedy or will it worsen my symptoms?

The world of nutrition and diets is built on beliefs. Every diet promotes its beliefs as the ultimate truth. But few of those who preach them have taken a moment to contemplate what a belief really is, and how it operates in our minds.

Here I recall the placebo effect: if you believe a sugar cube can heal you, it will. What that reveals is that our mind has far more power than we usually admit. And what does our mind do with that immense power? It enacts our beliefs, giving rise to our lived experience in perfect accordance with what we hold in our minds. If you believe the sugar cube will heal you, it will. Likewise, if you believe a certain food will harm you, it will. But it's not the food itself that has the power to heal or hurt us, it’s our mind. This, which is hard to understand and even harder to accept, is one of the keys to healing psychosomatic illness.

As I became increasingly aware of the mind’s limitless power, and of how my experience is simply a reflection of the beliefs I hold, I began to see how I had filled my mind with contradictory beliefs. These beliefs formed an algorithm in my mind that I would consult at every meal to decide what to eat. But because the beliefs were contradictory, the algorithm was incoherent. So every time I turned to it for guidance, my mind would fill with conflict, fear, and confusion. Those were the main ingredients of every meal I ate. No wonder they made me feel unwell.

This summer, in a particularly intense moment of crisis, one in which I no longer knew what to eat, I went on a four-day retreat. Naturally, I felt fear at the idea of entrusting my nutrition to someone else. Still, beneath the strident shouting of fear, a quiet Voice said, “trust.”

I arrived at the retreat site, a beautiful house on the shore of a reservoir. I met the two people who ran it, who radiated kindness. It didn’t take long for me to realize that these people, to whom I was handing over the keys to my nourishment, were instruments of Love.

On the first morning of the retreat, one of the participants gave a workshop in which she shared the wisdom she had gathered over her life in relation to food. At first, I felt resistance toward her and her teachings. Demons can’t stand the light. I realized this, and I breathed and tried to listen, opening my mind to the love this person was sharing. Meanwhile, from the kitchen downstairs, the smell of food rose. Like in a Disney movie, the aroma of pork belly frying floated up the stairs and brushed past my nose. My taste buds opened like flowers in the sun. Simultaneously, my mind reacted with rigidity and fear, trying to impose its severe law: “you will not take a single bite.” But this time, that voice didn’t sound so convincing. There was another voice: silent, present, much broader and kinder.

After the workshop, we went downstairs to eat. In the center of the table, a glorious platter of homemade pork belly stared at me, and I stared back at it.

If I had been in any other context, I truly wouldn’t have taken a bite. But this time, the context was different: in retreats, the communal intention invokes a powerful healing field. “For where two or three are gathered in my name, there am I in the midst of them,” said Love itself. Fear has no place there. So this time, despite supposedly being in the middle of a digestive crisis, despite having followed a vegan diet for almost two years, I picked up a piece of pork belly and, as if it were a divine gift, I brought it to my mouth.

I only ate one, and the flavor burst in my mouth like victorious trumpets.

Not a trace of fear or guilt. Only love, joy, and freedom. I had forgotten what it felt like to eat that way. It had to be a piece of pork belly that cracked open the wall and let the light through. But what was it that cracked?

After the meal, I approached I., the chef. I told him how my mind (and my diet) had just gloriously opened up. Then he said something like, “of course, man, pork belly fat is the easiest to digest.”

And in that moment, I understood the nature of the wall that had just cracked.

I realized that his beliefs about pork belly were diametrically opposed to the ones I held. But this time, I didn’t follow the usual ego’s route: to doubt him and try to impose my own beliefs as truth. This time, I doubted my entire structure of beliefs around food, and I saw that it was worth nothing. Without realizing it, I had built myself a prison, and the fact that all I could see were bars and dungeons didn’t make those beliefs true. It only confirmed that my mind was full of false beliefs, the kind that hide the truth and cause suffering.

Truth sets free. Beliefs restrict freedom; they sell it in exchange for lifeless formulas. The more beliefs, the more chains. My constant feeling of being trapped came from the accumulation of contradictory beliefs about what is good to eat. But in that moment, I doubted all of it, and that doubt revealed the illusory nature of that entire mental construct, of that narrative stitched together from secondhand knowledge. In that moment, I tasted the freedom and health that had never ceased to be mine, even though I had lost sight of them for years.

It’s been a month since that day, and the symptoms that had been driving me crazy for years are fading. The voice of fear still tempts me at every meal, reminding me of the sickly beliefs in my mind. Sometimes I still follow that voice, and suffering becomes the dessert.

But now I clearly hear that other voice: the spacious one, the one with no walls or formulas, the one that neither fears nor punishes, the one that reminds me of my innocence, the one that whispers: “I am at home. Fear is the stranger here.”
It is the voice of wisdom coming to the rescue. May we learn to listen and always follow it.

With all my love,

A.
