Throwing Stones at Nothing

Or learning to fill the absence of love with love

What do you feel when you look at the black part of this photo?

If you’re like me and have a human mind, you’ve probably felt a bit of unease.

Let’s analyze what’s happening there.

“Just because “nature abhors a vacuum,” which is true enough, it does not follow that a vacuum is filled with hellfire.” - A Course In Miracles

In reality, there are not two things: light and darkness. There is only light and its absence. Darkness has no qualities of its own; we’ve given it a name, but in truth, it is nothing.

The same goes for heat and cold. In truth, there is only one variable: heat. Cold is nothing more than the absence of that kinetic energy we experience as warmth.

Mystics speak of the fact that the only real thing is love, that all that exists is God. Yet, when we look at the world, there is no shortage of examples that seem to suggest the opposite.

But love has no opposite, just as heat or light have no opposite. Everything that appears as “opposite” is merely an absence, a vacuum that arises when what is real is blocked.

If we enter a dark room and want light, what do we do? Can you imagine how absurd it would be to shout at the shadow to go away? Or reject it, insult it, cancel it, throw stones at it, and tell it we don’t want it in our life?

Following that strategy, not only would the room remain dark, but our inner world would also sink into darkness.

In our minds, we would expend a huge amount of energy attacking that absence of light, but is it really possible to attack it? Can a stone truly harm something that is nothing?

Not only is the attack useless, it is impossible. All that one achieves in this way is to create a personal hell.

And after that first misstep, we make a second: we all want to be good people, and admitting that we carry a hell within us contradicts that virtuous identity. So we deny and repress that darkness, hiding it in the subconscious. From there, inevitably, it projects outward, and thus is born the cruel and fearful world we believe we see.

Now that hell seems to be outside, we feel it is beyond our control. We retreat into the role of the victim, and thus strengthen the chains that keep us suffering in the world of illusion.

This is how we humans exist by default: throwing stones at nothing.

And then comes Jesus, speaking of loving our enemies, or praying for those who wish us harm. Jesus was not interested in participating in our illusion (defeating the Romans, for example), but in freeing our minds from the internal error that gives rise to this entire mad world. His message, in essence, was: bring love to where you perceive its absence.

We have all heard of original sin: a supposed offense humanity committed at its origin; an attack on God that condemned it to a state of separation.

The fallacy that sustains this illusory state is that attack is a productive strategy, and, at a deeper level, that attacking or offending God is possible.

That is why a mantra I often use when I feel fear or under attack is: “I let go all my defenses.”For a moment, I feel panic; my ego shouts, “What are you doing? Raise your shield! Not the other cheek!” But invariably, after that instant of intense fear, the clouds part, and I see that I am completely safe. I always was. The fear came from defending myself against the supposed monsters that dwell in the dark, and I have just turned on the light.

This is true and works at all levels, with the darkness you perceive in yourself or in others. The solution is always the same: choose to fill the vacuum of love with love, not with attack.

The only exception I can think of is if you suddenly encounter a hungry tiger that wants to eat you. In that specific case, trust that fear, run, and fight for your life. In all other cases, doubt the reality of your fear, choose to let your defenses fall, renounce the attack, and watch how what once seemed a cruel world is revealed as Heaven on Earth.

With all my love,

A.
