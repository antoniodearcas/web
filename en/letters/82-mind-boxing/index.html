82. Mind Boxing

And the fruits of a trained mind

I am in my last semester, finishing my journey at Berklee. My classes are: one in music business, another on the relationship between music and spirituality, my senior project, and 14 hours of qigong every week. In truth, the only thing that really matters to me is qigong. The rest is fine and useful, but its value pales in comparison with what I learn through qigong.

Let me explain:

“Yoga is the cessation of the fluctuations of the mind.”
— Patañjali, Yoga Sūtras

The way to train the mind is by subjecting it to difficult situations without losing inner stillness. Yesterday in class we were doing an exercise called Yiquan (“mind boxing” in Chinese). As the name suggests, it is not an easy practice. It consists of holding a sequence of postures for a set amount of time, all done standing and in complete stillness. As this is done, the energy required to sustain the posture accumulates, and if one develops the skill to direct energy, it can be harvested. That is the essence of qigong: qi is energy, and gong is cultivation, or mastery.

Next to me was a friend who had arrived that day full of anger. He had just signed a contract that he considered unfair. This triggered his survival mechanisms, primarily anger (the desire to attack someone you perceive as an enemy in order to protect yourself). Anger, like all emotions, is a form of energy. If one learns to work with energy, one learns to work with emotions.

While practicing yiquan, my awareness caught the energy of anger radiating from my friend. Normally, when we are in the presence of someone carrying negative energy, our intuitive response is to move away. That is why, if we enter a subway car and see several empty seats, we instinctively choose to sit next to the person who seems light, cheerful, and peaceful, not next to the one fidgeting with anxiety, huffing in anger, or sobbing with grief.

“Then one is no longer disturbed by the dualities (pleasure and pain, success and failure, heat and cold).”
— Patañjali, Yoga Sūtras

This time, my first reaction was the usual one. I felt the energy of anger, and I felt rejection. I even identified with it, finding myself momentarily angry at my friend for bringing that energy. But quickly I saw the error, and I adopted a wider perspective: I accepted that the energy was there, and I saw that instead of resisting it, I could harvest it. And that is what I did. A few seconds later the energy harmonized, becoming much more pleasant. I felt relief, and I knew my friend felt it too. I realized then that I had just transcended resistance to negativity, using it to my advantage instead of letting myself be dragged into suffering.

Perhaps that is the meaning of “mental boxing”? It was like realizing I had spent my whole life in a ring, being hit from all sides, and finally standing up and taking the reins of the situation.

I am aware that all this may sound like the diary of an alien. I myself had no awareness of energy for most of my life. But in my second semester, after a couple of years of seriously experimenting with meditation, my mind relaxed so deeply that one day it opened to a new, subtler kind of perception.

At first, it was terrifying, like suddenly waking up in the middle of the ocean, at night, adrift and not knowing how to swim. But three years later, thanks to these qigong classes at Berklee, I am learning how to swim. That means that the negativity of the world has less and less power over me. Little by little, I am accepting and discovering that I am the cause of my experience, not an effect subjected to external causes. The truth I am cultivating is that nothing external has the power to affect my inner state unless I give it permission. The final fruit of a mind trained in this way is imperturbable peace, the peace that surpasses all understanding.

It is a long and difficult path, but I cannot think of anything better to devote my time to in this world. Business classes are useful and necessary for doing things properly. The senior project is a beautiful container into which I can pour the energy I have harvested. But the real work, the kind one carries beyond the grave, is to sit next to a friend burning with anger and learn to work with that energy for the liberation of both.

With all my love,

A.
