Nº 59

My first Teacher

Waves, eagles, leaves...

Yesterday I had the opportunity to play a couple of songs at an event called Earthapalooza, to celebrate Earth Day, which is this coming Tuesday. I took it as an invitation to reflect on my relationship with the Earth, and that’s what I want to share here today. At the same time, I’ve included a little gem about what to do with emotions, so I’m continuing that series as well. But first, a brief detour.

The epiphany I had at 21 taught me, without a doubt, that there is a divinity that encompasses everything and it’s essence is Love. I spent four hours in that Presence, feeling that Love and the perfection it pointed to. But when that state ended, it was as if a veil fell over my eyes. I lost sight of You, and I didn’t know how to return to that encounter.

So I went back to Church, but in none of my attempts did I find the Guide I was seeking, so I drifted away again. With no alternative in sight, I simply let it go, and little by little, I began to forget.

A few years later, after a period of crisis, I found myself on summer vacation by the sea. A friend had gifted me the Tao Te Ching, which I was reading attentively. Or more than reading it—I was contemplating it. I would read one of its poems each day, return to it several times, and let it linger in my mind. A shift began to happen in my consciousness. When I looked at water—whether in the sea or in a pond—I became absorbed. If I was at a cove, I’d read a Tao poem and then spend twenty minutes with my gaze fixed on the waves. I watched them come and go, interacting with one another. I saw how some went in one direction, others in another, and instead of crashing, they seemed to pass through each other. Something began to speak to me without words, and I returned whenever I could to listen. Without realizing it, the contemplative within me was awakening. And it awoke by becoming entranced with the movement of water.

A few months later, I traveled to India. The crisis that had begun a year earlier was threatening to turn into depression. But then that trip came, and I spent a week doing yoga and contemplating, in a house located right at the mouth of a river.

Without going into too much detail, the owner of the house turned out to be a sage, and he gave me surprising answers to questions like: “What’s worth thinking about?” or “How can I deal with emotions?” To the latter, he responded:

“The mind is like a computer: it has a CPU (Central Processing Unit) in charge of carrying out all operations. This CPU isn’t infinite; its capacity is limited. If we overload it with thoughts, there will be no space left for deeper processes.

You ask me about emotions because you feel an inner division, and that causes you pain. But thoughts are not the right tool for healing—they’re like a scalpel. A scalpel can’t be used to close a wound; using it will only cut further and create more fragmentation.

To heal that pain, you need to free up space in the CPU. Within you, there is a deeper intelligence that, once given room, will activate and resolve the division on its own, beyond the reach of your conscious mind.”

With those instructions—perfect for my engineer’s mind in search of spiritual healing—I began to practice. We started each day with three hours of yoga, and after breakfast, we would head to the beach.

I remember reconnecting with the sea. It felt like returning to a teacher. I knew that if I sat in front of it, it would speak to me again. So that’s what I did every day: after breakfast, I would go to the beach and sit in meditation at the shore, eyes fixed, in silent conversation with the waves that rhythmically washed over my legs. This time, I was also aware that each minute spent in that stillness was creating space for deeper healing processes.

With all that yoga, my mind was becoming increasingly calm, and my encounters more and more revealing. I remember realizing how, with each wave crashing into my body, it would then retreat into the ocean carrying my shape with it. Simply by sitting there, I was changing the ocean's form forever.

But by this point, it wasn’t only the sea that was speaking to me—the plants were, too. One of the last days of the retreat, I was walking back to the house along a dirt path through a forest. I remember looking at the vegetation around me, and in particular, being captivated by the leaves of a small shrub. That little shrub revealed a beauty I had never seen before and said to me: here is a small glimpse of the value of a quiet mind.

Toward the end of the trip, my mind was ready for deeper teachings, and one in particular arrived on the final morning before returning to Madrid. We were back in the city, on the rooftop of the hotel. Lying in the sun, five eagles appeared above me, gliding in circles, riding a column of warm air. Very slowly, my contemplative self opened its eyes, and what I saw was that death is an illusion. I now understand that, in that moment, I must have identified with something larger than this young man who writes to you now—and at that depth, I saw that life cannot die. “Energy is neither created nor destroyed, only transformed” also applies to life.

This week, as I recalled those first transcendent glimpses, I realized that the Earth was my first Teacher. What I didn’t find in Church, I found in the waves, in a shrub, or in a group of eagles. What I was seeking was communion—the encounter that happens when one recognizes oneself in something “external.” If it happens with a person, we call it love. If it’s with an object or with nature, we call it beauty. The words differ, but the experience is the same: you are me.

Beauty takes its time. So do love and understanding. My invitation for today is to look without hurry: if there are good clouds, take a moment to appreciate them (it’s a great massage for the mind). If you’re by the sea, sit at the shore and gaze. If it’s a shrub or a flower, contemplate a shrub or a flower. Everything in Nature is a symbol of the Divine. Give your CPU a break. Let that deeper intelligence take care of whatever needs healing. And in the meantime, enjoy the view.

I’ll finish with the last verse of a song I wrote during that trip to India. It’s called Nimbo, and it ends like this:

Isn’t it lovely, the heavenly song?

The world sings, if you like to listen

With all my love,

A.
