70. This is a happy moment

On how our search for happiness is what hides it.

One of the truths that Buddhism constantly emphasizes is that all living beings seek to secure the sources of happiness and eliminate the causes of suffering. We all do this 24 hours a day. If the whole planet is tirelessly working toward happiness, why does it seem that suffering is the rule and happiness the exception?

A clue lies in one of the central ideas of Socrates. He said that we always do what we perceive to be best (perception), but that rarely aligns with what truly is best (essence).

The problem lies in our perception. Our perception doesn’t show us reality; it shows us what we want to see. Our ego (that is, the beliefs we hold about the world) acts like a filter, like a pair of tinted glasses that distort our view. The denser the ego, the more negative the beliefs, the darker the glasses, and the darker the world appears. This is what in Sanskrit is called avidya, which literally means “ignorance” or “not knowing.” It doesn’t refer to intellectual ignorance, but to a mistaken perception of reality. It is a spiritual ignorance.

This avidya is what causes our efforts to find happiness and reduce suffering to often have the opposite effect.

Avidya shows us a world in which the sources of happiness lie outside of us. What we desire is, by definition, separate from us. And so, we devote entire lives to seeking happiness in experiences, substances, relationships, possessions, status—all kinds of things that are not inherent to who we are. The underlying belief is: “Something is missing in me, and I have to get it out there.”

What would happen if we took off the glasses and saw reality clearly?

That vision is ineffable, so it has been expressed in many different ways. But they all agree that suffering ends when we see clearly that what we were seeking—happiness, peace, love—is not found in things out there in the world, but are intrinsic qualities of our very Being. We already are the happiness we seek. Searching for it somewhere outside the Self is to deny its presence here and now. And in that denial, we feel miserable, empty. If we don’t know how to return to where it truly is, we’ll keep looking for it in the mirages of the world, drifting further and further from the source.

This vicious cycle traditionally ends when, after a great loss, one finally gets tired of seeking outside and turns inward.

An objection: right now, I’m in a park, listening to the birds and the gurgling of a fountain, writing with ease, surrounded by trees and flowers. Why do I feel happy here? Would I feel just as happy in a dark, windowless cell?

The answer is: it depends. It doesn’t depend on what’s out there, but on my inner posture. I remember traveling to wonderful places, surrounded by beloved and joyful people, and feeling deeply unhappy because I was in a state of internal conflict and pain. I also remember being on a very noisy, crowded subway, overwhelmed by sensory overstimulation, praying and surrendering completely to the experience without resistance—and experiencing one of the purest moments of happiness in my life. In neither case did my happiness depend on the place I was in, but rather on my capacity, in that moment, to say “yes” to whatever is in my experience in that moment.

Suffering is the activity of our mind when it resists reality. Happiness returns when we say “Yes” and surrender trustingly to the moment. Happiness is always present, like the blue sky, but our resistance to reality fills that sky with clouds.

Next time you find yourself in a happy moment (they all are, if we accept them as such), realize that in that moment, you are receiving your circumstances with open arms. That is what all happy moments have in common.

Next time you find yourself caught in suffering, whatever its form, realize that in that moment, you are intensely resisting whatever is happening.

In the face of that suffering, avidya will tell you that liberation lies in another time, in another place—leaving you abandoned here and now with your pain and confusion.

What I propose with this letter is that, in the face of such suffering, you stop whatever you are doing, close your eyes, and look inward for a while. Hold yourself in your suffering as a good friend would. Breathe. Doing this is already a huge step toward accepting reality. Reality is extremely simple—it is our imagination that fills it with complexity and fear. But all fears are illusions. By looking inward and not letting yourself be swept away by the narratives of the mind, you begin to cut through the agitation and return to the blue sky that awaits behind.

This is what we practice when we meditate. Meditation is not about controlling or silencing the mind. What it really seeks is to practice the capacity to be present—to open ever more fully to this moment. By doing that, the clouds begin to clear, and we start to see this moment as it truly is. If one practices this, happiness will soon begin to arise on its own from within. The only true happiness is that of our very existence. If we cultivate that, happiness can be found in any circumstance. That is what Buddha seems to remind us of with his gentle smile:

This is a happy moment.

With all my love,

A.
