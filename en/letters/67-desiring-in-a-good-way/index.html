67. Desiring in a Good Way

The work of saints and bodhisattvas

It’s not enough to desire good things. It’s also necessary to desire them in a good way.

I discovered this idea in the book Interior Peace by Jacques Philippe, and since then it has gradually illuminated an area of my life that, still in shadow, had long been a recurring source of suffering—for me and for those around me.

It’s natural to want to help someone we love. But if we do so from a place that is not peaceful—out of impatience, frustration, irritation, or blame—we end up adding suffering instead of relieving it.

What Jacques Philippe says on this matter is that such a desire to help is clearly not inspired by the Holy Spirit. He says our desire must come from a peaceful place—from a serene and patient love—in order to offer real help.

The Holy Spirit is the response God gives to the voice of the ego. Where the ego perceives evil and demands conflict, the Holy Spirit sees innocence and offers peace. The Holy Spirit is, therefore, the good Guide.

Lately, as I return to Buddhist teachings, I’ve begun to see the deep convergence between the figure of the Holy Spirit and the purpose of the Buddhist path: to see reality as it is, without distortion, without judgment, without illusion.

This vision manifests in three ways:

  • Wisdom (prajña): the direct understanding that everything is interconnected; that the suffering of another is also my own. It is also the realization that the “separate self” that thinks it suffers is an illusion—an error in perception that makes separation, guilt, sin, and fear seem real.

  • Clear vision (vipassana): the calm observation of what arises, without clinging or rejecting anything, letting everything be just as it is. This requires full surrender to the present moment, releasing the ego’s desire for control.

  • Bodhicitta: the sincere wish to free all beings from suffering through love, compassion, and the commitment to awakening. Like the Holy Spirit, it sees no enemies—only sleeping brothers.

In Christianity, someone who embodies these three qualities is called a saint. In Buddhism, a bodhisattva. Though they come from very different theologies, in practice they are very similar: human beings who have remembered their true nature and learned to live in alignment with it. Both are capable of acting from a clear vision, a loving will, and a peaceful mind. Both have consciously cultivated the ability to desire the good in a truly good way.

With all my love,

A.
