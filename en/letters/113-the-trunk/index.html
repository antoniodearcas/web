113. The Trunk

Too many branches to tend to? Find the trunk.

In a world designed for specialists, generalists like me can easily fall into the trap of "jack of all trades, master of none."

In this letter I share a great eureka moment that has unified, all at once, all the branches of my activity. From many things to do, to just one.

"Don't plan, let things happen for you."

With those words, my qigong teacher proposed a path for me to follow on my return to Madrid.

Five months later, my weeks are filled with activities that feel deeply aligned with my path. I didn't seek any of them. They simply came to me and I said "okay, I'm in." That's how friends, mentors, clients, partners and projects have arrived.

Despite the evident harmony of everything that's unfolding, these past few weeks I had the feeling that I was saying yes to too many things.

This week, however, I had a great eureka moment that showed me how, despite the apparent multiplicity of my activities, I am really only doing one thing.

When I saw this, I shared my discovery with Betül, who once again shone with one of her one-liners: "It's like a tree, and you've discovered the trunk."

She was right. If I felt like I was taking on too much, it was because I was putting my attention on the branches, trying to make them grow and bear fruit. From that angle, I felt all the branches competing for my attention. There never seems to be enough time or energy to nourish everything.

But all the branches converge in a trunk. And if I tend to the trunk, the branches receive what they need without me having to think about each one individually.

The trunk is the common denominator, the critical point of the system. The one place where, if I direct my attention, all other areas are affected.

The key to all of this lies in meaning. The branches represent not only different activities and areas of my life, but the purpose each one serves. When I look at the branches, each seems to have its own purpose: cooking to care for my body, creating content to reach more people, practicing music to cultivate my gift, doing qigong to keep my energy clean. It's impossible not to feel, from that perspective, that we are doing too many things. The purpose of my life seems to be divided into multiple facets, always competing with one another.

But there is a deeper purpose that sustains them all, the trunk that unifies and nourishes every branch: the trunk is, simply, to be happy. That is the central purpose that unifies all purposes.

What if, instead of doing each activity with its individual function in mind, I did it with that common purpose in mind?

What if instead of washing the dishes to keep the house clean, I did it to be happy while washing the dishes?

The difference is radical. Instead of forcing my mind to fulfill a thousand different functions, I focus it on just one.

Now, when I say be happy, I don't mean the temporary happiness that the things of this world offer. I mean the happiness that shines when I know myself, and I experience myself, as loved. It’s the joy of being aware that God's love is present, and that the entire universe sustains me in whatever I am doing.

Little by little my mind is opening to that reality that transcends my individual world, returning me to the awareness of that love which unites all things.

And how does one open to that loving presence?

By forgiving.

So we could take one step closer to the essence of the matter. The central purpose is not to be happy, but to forgive. Forgiveness opens the source of happiness. It cleans the window so that light can come through. Without forgiveness, resentments seem to deny the presence of love, and being happy is impossible without love.

The forgiveness I'm talking about is not moral forgiveness. It is letting go of everything that is not love.

Looking at that thought charged with negativity, recognizing its illusory nature and saying: "Holy Spirit, this is taking my peace away, and I want peace. I ask you to take this from me."

That is all I have to do, while washing the dishes, while driving, while making the bed, while using my phone, while writing or singing. That always takes me back to happiness, flow and ease.

From there, all things happen and arrange themselves on their own.

From there, it is no longer I who washes the dishes. When I align with love, my identity aligns with Love. I then experience what Paul expressed as "it is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me." What I experience is that I have stepped aside, and Christ has stood up, and it is He who washes the dishes, for me, with all His Love.

With all my love,

Antonio
