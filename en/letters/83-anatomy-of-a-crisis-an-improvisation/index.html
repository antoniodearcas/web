83. Anatomy of a Crisis: an Improvisation

And the story of a dear melody

A couple of weeks ago, I sent out a letter exploring through words what the experience of a crisis feels like. Today, I’m sharing with you an expression of the same theme through piano and dance.

Naomi and I are together in a class called Music, Spirit & Transformation, where we’ve been studying the music of composers like Arvo Pärt and Sofia Gubaidulina. A couple of weeks ago, we were asked to create an original composition in a similar vein. I had just written the letter “81. Anatomy of a Crisis,” so I immediately saw how to reflect that process of death and rebirth through the piano. I approached Naomi, shared my idea with her, and a few days later this improvisation emerged. This is the first take.

There’s only one part that wasn’t improvised: the four-note melody I sing at the beginning and at the end. That melody has a beautiful story.

There were a few years when I practically lost the ability to sing. Whenever the opportunity to perform arose, a pharyngitis would take away my instrument. This would happen 3 or 4 times a year. So for four years, I didn’t play live or record anything.

Being at Berklee under those conditions was very hard. I had voice lessons and was required to sing in different ensembles. Once I even had to sing Led Zeppelin... I always felt I had to force my voice, and fear became a constant companion.

But my journey at Berklee wasn’t really about making music, it was about seeking truth. So one day, I finally decided to stop forcing my voice to do things it didn’t want to do. Then came the fear: the fear of failing my classes or of never singing again if I didn’t push myself. But fear is always illusory.

What happened next was that, a couple of days later, an Indian friend showed me how he practiced with his voice: he would play a drone, a single sustained note that lasts for a long time, and improvise over it.

I decided to try it, still keeping the intention not to force myself in any way. I put on the drone in the background, sat down with my hands resting on my legs, palms open, prayed for guidance, and then brought my attention to my throat. I began softly chanting "om," feeling the vibration of my vocal cords. And suddenly, a melody appeared: from C to G, and from F to E. As I sang that melody, I felt as if I had just been embraced. I kept singing it, observing the effect it had on my throat. After a couple of minutes, I began to cry, and I knew that melody had helped me release the emotional energy that had been stuck there.

The next day, I repeated the practice, with the same result.

When I arrived at my singing class, I told my teacher, "I’ve decided I’m not going to sing my exercises or the Chet Baker song anymore. I just want to sing this." And my teacher, a man with an open heart, accepted my path and joined my singing, adding harmonies.

For two weeks, I sang only that melody. Little by little, I was able to go longer without breaking into tears, until one day I finally felt joy in singing again. That mantra helped me pass through the clouds that had darkened my voice.

The improvisation with Naomi reflects that process, ending in the same way the real crisis did: by singing that simple and peaceful melody. Although in reality, that mantra wasn’t the end, it was the beginning of my healing process. A journey that would culminate a few months later with the miraculous intervention of my qigong teacher. But that’s another story.

With all my love,

A.
