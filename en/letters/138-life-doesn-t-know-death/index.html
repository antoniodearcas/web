138. Life Doesn't Know Death

When poets celebrate the freedom of birds, they're really talking about the sky.

It was night. Muhammet, Betül's brother, and I were looking out in silence from the balcony. Hills of concrete and thousands of lit windows stretching toward the horizon in every direction: Istanbul. “Dystopia,” Muhammet said. I nodded, and we lost ourselves in the distance again.

Yesterday Gülistan, Betül’s mother, had surgery. It was a hard day, listening to death drag its pale feet through the hospital corridors.

I was sitting in an armchair, taking the pulse of the room. There was fear, sadness, exhaustion, anger. I still can’t understand a word of Turkish, but I’m fluent in Energy.

I looked out the window and saw a few flocks of birds flying together, having the time of their lives. Then a thought came to me that I had never had before:

Life doesn’t know death.

I was grateful for the gift, a moment worthy of a haiku. In fact, here it is:

Last dance
Between swifts and sky
Dark clouds

That thought mirrored the paradox I had been living all day. We have two overlapping natures: one bound by time and space, the other eternal and infinite.

Swift and sky.

I think that when poets celebrate the freedom of birds, they are really talking about the sky. But since there is little to say about it, we speak of those who know it best.

Jesus and God.

Once you recognize this seemingly dual nature, it becomes clear that death touches only one of them. The other is Life itself.

Then you look at the devastation that is inseparable from this world, and inside, you don’t move, at least not deep down. You look at it and tell it: you are not real, and you cannot touch me, at least not deep down.

This is not intellectual, it is not a belief. Before that thought arrived, “Life doesn’t know death,” what I felt was an unshakable peace in the midst of the dark clouds. An indifference that Saint Ignatius described not as apathy or insensitivity, but as inner freedom. I experienced it as compassion, which says to the one who suffers: I see your suffering, I give it room to be, but I also recognize your freedom.

Fear passed through me yesterday, but it couldn’t grab hold of me and carry me off. My role was a different one: to hold on to that peace and bring it to the surface. The swift is individual, the sky is shared. All it takes is for one swift to cry out “we’re flying!” for all of them to realize the infinity that embraces and holds them.

That is my song today, which I express in these words: Life doesn’t know death!

A few minutes ago, Gülistan came out of intensive care and returned to her room. While her children look after her, I write this, like a seismograph recording spiritual tremors.

I don’t know to what extent Gülistan identifies with the sky she already is. What I do know is that the first words she murmured when she opened her eyes and saw her daughter were: I was with my father and my mother, and we were going to Mecca.

With all my love,

Antonio
