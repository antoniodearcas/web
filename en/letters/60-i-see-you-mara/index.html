60. I see you, Mara

Construction and deconstruction of a world.

The other day, during Qigong class, J. invited us to say something that wasn’t true. The idea was that, in the purity of the energy we had cultivated, telling a lie would be like a drop of black ink falling into a glass of water. The invitation was to try to perceive, energetically, that lack of truth. It was an exercise in discernment.

My classmate Olivia said her name was Marta. We all "listened" in silence. The boy next to her said he hadn't had breakfast. Another said he was wearing pink underwear. The effect was subtle but noticeable — like a thin cloud momentarily dimming the light of the Moon.

When my turn came, my mind — being as it is — said, “My being is separate from the Being of God.” Immediately, a wave of nausea came over me. J. looked down and said, “I have to look down. Say something to correct it.” I then said, “My being and the Being of God are One.” That seemed to restore the energy in the class, but something had stirred within me, and I spent the rest of the day feeling truly uncomfortable.

What I realized was that this lie is the foundational belief of the universe we think we live in. Being aware that my mind builds my perception of the world based on my beliefs, I saw that the cornerstone of my belief system was that lie. The world we perceive, made up of separate things, is a denial of the Truth.

The crisis that experience unleashed ended up forcing my sense of identity to leap beyond the world. “In the world, but not of the world” gained a little more meaning. The death of the ego ceased to seem the death of myself. I still forget this constantly, but in a deeper place in my mind, that truth is now firmly established and never fully leaves my awareness.

From that place, I wrote this poem:

I embrace myself

In this storm of lights

I return my name to silence

And my hands are no longer my hands

Nor do my words summon nightmares

I am what I am

Before my chains and conflicts

Prior to the lily’s root

I deny my authorship

This was not written by the human

Because the human did not write the human

Let go of that dreadful idea

And let death die of embraces

Art is a dream that awakens (to) life

Today I want to explore some of the effects of believing in that dreadful idea, as I call it in the poem.

Believing that there is a separation between your being and God (also known as "All That Is") immediately creates an illusory separate self: the ego. The ego is the belief in separation, along with all the other beliefs that stem from it. Imagine a wave denying the existence of the ocean (God) and of water itself (Spirit). If someone were to tell this wave, "You are water!" its ego would reply, "No! I’m Billy!"

It also generates a world of separate objects. God is the water from which all waves derive their substance — the Being from which all things derive their being. If God is denied, what remains is a world of orphaned, hollow things, disconnected from each other. Each wave sees itself as something independent, instead of an expression of the ocean. The same happens with people, each seemingly isolated in their mind, in their body. Everything becomes a discrete, separate “thing.” Everything loses its sense of belonging. The One fragments into ten thousand. Subjectively, it feels like being in exile, without knowing where our true home is.

From a psychological point of view, the ego appears in this world of separation and thinks:

"I am the body."

"I must protect myself."

"I feel incomplete, something is missing — I don't know what — but I must search for it in the world and get it before others take it from me."

The entire drama of human existence — all wars, all tales of romance, ambition, revenge — all are born from this idea of separation and the thought system that springs from it. The world is a theater where each mind deals, in its own way, with that mistaken belief.

From an emotional point of view, this separation gives rise to three fundamental emotions.

First, a longing for something greater than oneself. Some seek it in a partner, in a family; others in more success, more money, more experiences, more travel, more clothes, more sexual partners, more chocolate, more muscle mass. All of these are, in reality, the irresistible call of Love drawing us back home. It reminds me of this beautiful quote by Saint Augustine:

"You have made us for Yourself, O Lord, and our heart is restless until it rests in You."

At the same time, that longing for union terrifies us. Now that we are identified with a separate self, returning to union implies the death of what we believe ourselves to be (the ego). From there, fear arises in all its forms. The fear of intimacy, the fear of being vulnerable, of showing ourselves authentically. The fear of surrendering to something eternal. The fear of choosing and thus losing the deceptive freedom of having a thousand options. The fear of the mysterious, the unknown. The fear of death, which manifests every time we doubt ourselves, every time we feel anxiety about what might happen, the fear of losing control, the fear of illness and aging, the fear of stopping, the fear of acting, the fear of loss, the fear of looking inward. Ultimately, it is the fear of Love.

In third place on the podium of emotions stands guilt. Buried in the subconscious, we all carry the guilt of having stolen a piece of God Himself. The guilt of having profaned something sacred. This guilt is so immense that the ego cannot accept it and projects it outward. From there arises the idea of a punishing God — another absurdity of the ego, for God is only Love, compassion, and mercy. Yet the ego believes it is going to be punished. So, in yet another absurd twist, it decides to punish itself before God does, hoping to appease God’s wrath. The ancients offered animal sacrifices. We, more modern, choose to suffer, to fall ill, to sabotage ourselves. We also blame others and ourselves.

But alongside that guilt, also hidden deep within the subconscious, remains the memory of union — the memory of feeling whole, of being at home, at peace, safe from all harm. There remains the memory that God loves us exactly as we are now. The problem is not our supposed imperfection (or the world's); the problem is that we believe we are separate.

Remembering this union with God is not like recalling a memory from last summer. Remembering that union is allowing God to pass through our heart once again, re-cognising our union now, in the eternal now. This will reveal that the "dreadful idea," the cornerstone upon which the ego is built, is false. It is the wave having the experience of being water. The ego will resist this tooth and nail. Its hegemony over your mind depends on it — keeping it busy with the past and the future (both entirely imaginary) to keep the simple truth out of awareness: Ah, all that I am is water! How could I have missed this?! And who or what on earth is Billy!?

You are not the ego; you are not that person who was born and will die. You are spirit, a Child of God: invulnerable, innocent, eternally safe, one with all that is. But it is precisely that innocence that has been deceived by the ego’s stories.

Now, the ego is not an enemy. Only the ego has enemies — and you are spirit. There is no need to fight it or defeat it: it is enough to look at it serenely, recognize its falseness, and move on.

The process of remembering what we are consists in ceasing to believe in what is not true; in ceasing to give credibility to the voice of the ego. When you wake up in the morning, what seemed completely real within the dream is suddenly recognized for what it is — just a dream. This world is the same, only that here one awakens slowly from within the dream. That is why, in Qigong class, we are training the ability to perceive what is false; we are waking up. This world only begins to make sense when it is understood as a school for choosing the true over the false; as a tool for awakening.

To finish, two examples of elder brothers who have already graduated from this school, leaving behind their notes and an earpiece in case any exam becomes particularly difficult:

The story goes that, sitting beneath the Bodhi tree, the Buddha was tempted by Mara (another symbol of the ego). Mara hurled storms, armies of demons, beautiful dancing women, and deep doubts at him. The Buddha gazed serenely and said: "I see you, Mara," and there he attained Enlightenment.

A very similar story is that of Jesus and his journey through the desert. There, Jesus faces Satan (another symbol of the ego). Satan tempts him with hunger, provokes him to test God, and offers him power over the world. Jesus responds to these temptations in various ways, but the message is always the same: "No, thank you; I am of God, and that is enough."

Just as Siddhartha and Jesus recognized the illusion and chose not to believe in it, so can we. Buddha and Christ are your true names. Although, in truth, what you are cannot be named — for to name it would be to limit it, and you have no limits.

To every thought of fear, I say: "No, thank you."

To every temptation to fill an inner emptiness with something from the world, I say: "I am of God, and that is enough."

To the voice that tells me I am guilty, that I can’t, that I don’t deserve to be loved or happy, I say: "I see you, Mara."

And I rest in the truth of what I am: unchanging, invulnerable, patient.

And if things get ugly, I shout: 'Jesus Christ, help! Give me your hand, Lord!'

And I hug myself as the storm of lights passes.

With all my love,

A.
