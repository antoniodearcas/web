Nº 72 · July 20, 2025

Beyond good and evil, there is a field

What makes something negative?

It is part of our nature to have to make decisions at every instant. Some decisions lead us to better places, with more harmony, freedom, and peace. Others take us in the opposite direction. This is what some religious approaches simplify with the terms “good” and “evil,” offering moral prescriptions that one then tries to obey. “Stabbing someone is very bad!” they might say. “Saving someone’s life is very good!” as well.

The problem with that approach is that reality is much more complex, and the meaning of an action does not depend on the action itself, but on its context, which is the same as intention. A surgeon, for example, might use a knife (a scalpel) to cut someone open and save their life. That’s why I increasingly distrust these simplified formulas often offered by religions, and instead seek to guide my decisions based on my own wisdom and discernment.

Rumi’s quote comes to mind: “Beyond good and evil, there is a field. I’ll meet you there.”

Truly enlightened beings do not speak in terms of “this is good” and “this is bad.” They simply know the reality of things and choose what they know to be most favorable, without the need for beliefs, judgments, or condemnations.

A great example of this is the Buddha. Instead of proclaiming what is moral or immoral, virtuous or harmful, good or bad, the Buddha simply observed the true nature of the phenomenal world and described it. He did not offer a list of prescriptions for what we should do, but rather taught discernment, appealing to our own observation and wisdom. He taught how to recognize whether something is negative, using four criteria.

The first criterion is that negative actions and their root (neuroses centered on a personal self) cause suffering. They are painful in our direct experience: unloading our anger onto someone, acting from lack and craving, judging others, giving in to envy or pride, being confused about what we should do… none of these states are pleasant or peaceful. These states are called kleshas (literally “poisons”), and they are painful, sometimes extremely so.

The second criterion is that any activity arising from a klesha veils the truth. A fundamental truth (though still relative) is that all beings seek happiness and work to secure its sources. They also seek to alleviate their suffering and avoid its causes. Absolute Truth, on the other hand, is non-dual. In that realm, there is no personal self separated from others. Absolute Truth is the One Self—God, Brahman, or whatever name one chooses to give to that ineffable reality.

When our mind is absorbed in a klesha, both relative and absolute truth become completely obscured. Recognizing the unity and innocence of all beings is the last thing we care about when we’re angry. All we want is to be right, to win, and to get what we’re fighting for. Truth is experienced as perfect peace and balance, but the kleshas cover it with a veil of painful agitation. It is forgotten as if it had never existed. As A Course in Miracles says: “It is impossible to see two worlds at once.”

The third criterion is that negative neuroses tend to multiply and reinforce their own kind. If, for example, I give in to a temptation one day, it becomes much easier to fall into it again the next. If I let myself indulge in laziness today, I’m more likely to repeat the pattern tomorrow.

Desire itself is insatiable. Trying to satisfy it by obeying its impulses is like trying to quench thirst with salty water, it only leads to more thirst. This insatiable mental state of craving is called sépa in Tibetan, and it intensifies every time we act from it. Our culture heavily feeds this state of sépa, leading to an endless proliferation of desires and a perpetual sense of dissatisfaction.

The fourth and final criterion is that any form of negativity we engage in (whether in thought, word, or deed) plants a seed in our mind. When the right conditions arise, that seed will sprout as a form of suffering, whether in the mind, the body, or the environment. Imagine a single seed giving rise to a great tree, full of fruit laden with similar seeds.

As you can see, it is not beyond our reach to recognize from our own experience whether something is good or bad. Moralistic beliefs lead to fear and guilt, both daughters of unconsciousness. Direct observation, on the other hand, leads to love and peace, which are essential qualities of awareness. I wish for you to observe your life in this way, not through filters and formulas, but directly. Replacing dogma with contemplation, fear with wisdom, and guilt with integrity.

Or, in a single phrase, a paraphrase of Saint Paul’s letter to the Corinthians:

“All things are lawful for me, but not all things are expedient: all things are lawful for me, but not everything is constructive; all things are lawful for me, but I will not be enslaved by anything.”

With all my love,

A.
